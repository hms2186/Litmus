Ocean Relief
As we explore human–ocean connections, let us begin with the
ocean floor and its features. Just as land has mountains, valleys, and
plains, the ocean floor, too, has several landforms, together known
as submarine relief or ocean relief. These include underwater
mountains, valleys, plateaus, and trenches. The Mariana Trench
in the Pacific Ocean is the deepest place on Earth, while the
Mid-Ocean Ridge is the world’s longest mountain range, hidden
beneath the ocean. Some underwater mountains like Mauna Kea
(Hawai‘i) are even taller than Mount Everest.

Movement of Ocean Water—Waves,
Tides, and Currents
The water in the oceans is constantly in motion. Waves, tides, and
currents are not merely movements of ocean water; they help shape
the Earth’s surface, support marine life, aid fishing and travel,
and even influence climate. Let us learn more about these oceanic
movements.
Waves
When the surface of the ocean rises and falls alternately, it is called
a wave. Waves are primarily caused by winds blowing across the
water. They carry energy across the oceans over long distances.
Observe Fig. 1.3 and note the crests (high peaks) and troughs (lower
valleys).
Waves are often called coastal architects as they shape the shoreline
through erosion and sediment transport. Observe the various
landforms formed by waves in Fig. 1.4 and note how wave action
creates erosional features, such as sea cracks, caves, arches, sea stacks,
and stumps. Also notice the original headland shown in the figure.
In addition to erosional landforms, waves also form depositional
features such as beaches.

Tides
Tides are the rhythmic rise and fall of ocean water that occur
twice a day in most parts of the world. When the water rises to
its highest level and covers much of the shore, it is called high tide
(flood tide). When the water falls to its lowest level and moves
away from the shore, it is called low tide (ebb tide). Observe Fig. 1.5
and note the change in water level during high and low tides. Do
you notice the oval layer around the Earth indicating water level?
Can you also see how the water level rises during high tide and falls
during low tide?

High tides are important for navigation because they raise the water
level near the shore, allowing ships to enter harbours more easily.
In ports and coastal areas, tidal information is essential, as tidal
ranges in some ports can reach 4–5 metres. The Gulf of Khambhat
has one of the highest tidal ranges, at about 12 metres. Before a ship
berths at a port, it must check both the water depth and the tidal
range to ensure safe docking, especially during low tide.
Some sea channels, such as the Singapore Strait, contain shallow
areas. Hence, ships such as Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs)
passing through these regions often wait for hours to transit only
during high tide to ensure sufficient water depth and avoid running
aground.
Tidal changes also generate strong currents because large volumes
of water flow in and out of coastal areas. These tidal currents can
reach speeds of 5–6 knots (about 9–11 km/h).

Ocean Currents
Ocean currents are continuous streams of water that flow across
the ocean surface in definite directions (Fig. 1.9). They are caused
by factors, such as winds, the Earth’s rotation (Coriolis force),
differences in temperature and salinity, and the shape of continents
and ocean basins. Ocean currents may be warm or cold depending
on the direction from which the water flows. Currents flowing from
warmer regions are called warm currents, while those flowing from
colder regions are called cold currents. On maps, warm currents are
usually shown in red and cold currents in blue.

The Gulf Stream, a warm current originating in the Gulf of Mexico
in the Atlantic Ocean, flows towards Norway and keeps its waters
relatively warm. As a result, sea ice does not form easily in the
Norwegian region despite its high latitude (60°–70°). In contrast,
the Labrador Current, a cold current flowing along the east coast of
Canada, carries freezing water and supports the formation of sea ice
and icebergs even at lower latitudes.

Cyclones and Tsunamis—Early Warning
Systems
Oceans and seas can give rise to sudden natural hazards such as
cyclones, which are large rotating storms, and tsunamis, which are
giant waves caused by earthquakes. To protect people from such
dangers, scientists have developed Early Warning Systems that work
like giant alarm systems for coastal areas. To reduce risks, people,
communities, and governments must act in advance. Early Warning
Systems help detect cyclones and tsunamis, predict disasters, and
issue timely warnings so that people can evacuate and stay safe.

Marine Resources and Their Significance
Do you recall learning about natural resources in the Grade 8 Social
Science Part 1 textbook? These resources are obtained from land,
water, forests and animals, and other natural sources. Oceans, too,
are vast storehouses of resources, and the materials and features
found in oceans that have intrinsic or economic value are called
marine resources. Marine resources can be broadly classified into
two categories—Marine Living Resources and Marine Non-living
Resources. They include fish and seafood, biodiversity, oil and
natural gas, minerals, sand and gravel, renewable blue energy
sources such as tidal and wave energy, tourism potential, and
unique ecosystems such as coral reefs. Fig. 1.17 presents a detailed
classification of marine resources.
Significance of Marine Resources
Marine resources are vital for food security, economic activities, and
maintaining a healthy environment (Fig. 1.17). Countries with long
coastlines, such as India, benefit greatly from the sea. Fishing, trade,
and tourism provide livelihoods to millions of people, while oceans
also support biodiversity and sustain numerous plant and animal
species. However, marine resources must be used responsibly
without disturbing marine ecosystems.

Maritime Zones
Have you ever wondered who owns the oceans and how
countries decide where they can fish or travel? To understand this,
we need to learn about maritime zones—different areas of the ocean
defined by international law.
The sea area closest to a country is called its territorial waters, over
which the country has near-complete control, subject to the right of
innocent passage for foreign ships. Just as on land, the government
can make laws relating to customs, immigration, and pollution
control in this zone.
Observe Fig. 1.20 and note the extent of the territorial sea in nautical
miles. Beyond this lies the contiguous zone, extending from the
baseline up to 24 nautical miles, where the country has limited
control over customs, fiscal matters, and illegal immigration.

Innocent Passage is a principle that allows foreign ships to pass
through the territorial waters of a country as long as they do
not threaten its peace, good order, or security. Ships must move
continuously without stopping, except in emergencies or cases of
breakdowns. This right does not permit activities such as fishing,
military operations, or other non-peaceful actions.
Beyond the contiguous zone, lie the Exclusive Economic Zones
(EEZs), which extend from the baseline up to 200 nautical miles from
the coast, and are defined under the United Nations Convention on
the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) by global agreement. In these zones, a
country has special rights to use ocean resources, such as fish, oil,
and gas, although ships from other countries may still pass through
peacefully. It is important to note, however, that the country does not
fully own or control the waters of the EEZ.
Beyond the EEZs of all countries lie the high seas, also known as
international waters. Covering about 64 per cent of the world’s
oceans, these waters do not belong to any single country and are
therefore considered part of the global commons. All countries enjoy
equal rights on the high seas, including navigation, fishing, flying
over them, laying undersea cables, building lawful structures such
as artificial lands, and exploring for minerals.

Although the high seas are essential, they also present many
challenges. Since no single country controls them, issues, such
as overfishing, illegal fishing, and conflicts over resources often
arise. These problems affect millions of people who depend on
the sea for food and livelihoods. Ensuring navigation and safety is
also more difficult without clear enforcement mechanisms. On the
high seas, the laws of the flag state—the country in which a ship is
registered—apply.
International Maritime Rules and Regulations
In the earlier sections of this chapter, you learnt that oceans are
vital for trade, travel, and communication. To ensure that activities
at sea remain safe, fair, and peaceful, countries follow a set of
international rules and agreements known as maritime law or
maritime regulations. These laws regulate how ships operate and
move; protect the marine environment; address issues such as
piracy, terrorism, natural disasters, and accidents; safeguard trade
routes; and help settle disputes between countries.

Important Maritime Rules and Regulations
Just as there are traffic rules on land, ships must follow “rules of

(Convention on the International Regulations for Preventing

1 – Oceans
and Life
Ships must also meet safety standards and are prohibited from
polluting the seas. The MARPOL (International Convention for the
Prevention of Pollution from Ships) Convention is an important
international agreement aimed at reducing pollution from ships.
Why are Maritime Rules Important?
International maritime rules, regulations, and agreements help keep
the oceans safe and ensure the fair and sustainable use of ocean
resources. These rules enable countries to cooperate in using marine
resources without conflict while protecting marine life, ships, sailors,
and coastal communities. Without such regulations, the oceans could
become dangerous and chaotic, with increased pollution, accidents,
collisions, and disputes over resources. By establishing common
standards for all ships, regardless of the country they belong to,
maritime rules help keep the oceans safe, clean, and open for trade
and travel.
Before we move on…
Æ Oceans are vital for global trade, travel, communication, and
climate regulation.
Æ Submarine relief refers to the physical features of the ocean,
such as the continental shelf, ridges, trenches, and seamounts,
which influence marine life, resource availability, and
undersea exploration.
Æ Movements of ocean water—waves, tides, and currents—affect
marine ecosystems, global climate, and human activities like
fishing and navigation.
Æ Maritime zones include territorial waters, EEZs (Exclusive
Economic Zones), and the high seas.
Æ Sustainable use of marine resources through cooperation,
regulation, and conservation is essential to protect aquatic life
and support future generations.
Æ Maritime rules and regulations exist to resolve disputes over
marine boundaries.
Æ International laws, such as UNCLOS (United Nations
Convention on the Law of the Sea), help define maritime zones
and settle disputes among countries.
